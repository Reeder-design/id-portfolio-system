How I Got Here

Education, technology, and a lot of curiosity.

I actually started my career as a high school history and English teacher.

That experience still has a huge influence on the way I design learning. Teaching made me think a lot about what people really understand versus what they've simply been exposed to, how to scaffold difficult information, how to give useful feedback, and how to tell whether an assessment is actually measuring what you intended it to measure.

I eventually moved from classroom education into instructional design and corporate learning. That gave me the chance to combine the part of education I loved with something else I've always been interested in: technology.

Since then, my work has expanded into digital learning development, sales enablement, LMS administration, learning analytics, automation, AI evaluation, and custom interactive development.

I also hold a Master of Education, so my approach tends to live somewhere between learning theory, practical instructional design, and figuring out how technology can make the whole thing work better.

How I Think About Learning

A few things I keep coming back to.

Start with what people need to do.

I don't like starting with, “What slides should we make?” I'd rather start with what someone should actually be able to do differently afterward.

Let people practice.

I'm a big believer in scenarios, decisions, application, and feedback. If someone has to use the information later, the learning should give them a chance to practice using it.

Make complexity manageable.

My job isn't to turn every piece of SME knowledge into training. It's to figure out what the learner actually needs and organize it in a way that makes sense.

Build things that can be reused.

If I know I'm going to need something again, I'd rather build a template, component, workflow, or system than rebuild it from scratch every time.

Good learning has to work.

I care about polish, but I also care about accuracy, usability, accessibility, technical functionality, instructional alignment, and whether the learner can actually use it.

What I'm Learning Now

More control over what I can build.

Right now, one of my biggest professional development projects is learning how to build custom web-based learning components.

I'm creating a private library of HTML, CSS, and JavaScript templates, interaction patterns, Storyline and Rise assets, design systems, scoring logic, and development workflows that I can reuse in future projects.

I'm not trying to become a software engineer instead of an instructional designer. I'm interested in understanding enough development to have more control over what I can build as an instructional designer.

I also want to understand what AI-generated code is actually doing instead of treating it like a magic box.

The Common Thread

Different projects. Same question.

I might be designing a sales certification one day, cleaning LMS data with Python another day, reviewing an AI-generated artifact, or figuring out how to make JavaScript talk to a learning interaction.

But the question underneath all of it is usually: How can I make this clearer, more useful, and easier for someone to actually use?
