Overview

The Work

My AI-related work has included model training, output evaluation, content review, data annotation, iterative feedback, and testing AI-generated professional content.

I've worked across platform-based AI training, freelance projects, internal testing, and AI-assisted instructional design workflows.

While the specific models, prompts, datasets, and client materials used in this work are confidential, the core evaluation process is something I can demonstrate.

The Challenge

A polished answer isn't always a good answer.

One of the biggest challenges in AI evaluation is that model output can sound confident, professional, and complete while still containing important problems.

Those problems aren't always obvious factual errors. They can also include missing requirements, weak reasoning, poor organization, ambiguous instructions, inconsistent terminology, inappropriate assumptions, or an answer that technically responds to the prompt but isn't actually useful.

Evaluation Question: Is this output simply convincing, or is it accurate, aligned, complete, usable, and appropriate for the person who actually needs it?

Evaluation Context

What I was evaluating.

Depending on the project, evaluation could involve reviewing generated responses, professional artifacts, learning content, structured tasks, or model behavior against a defined set of criteria.

My instructional design background is especially useful when the output involves learning, communication, documentation, or professional workflows because I'm already used to separating surface polish from actual quality.

Evaluation Goals

What was I looking for?

  • Identify factual, logical, structural, or instructional errors.
  • Determine whether the output actually followed the user's requirements.
  • Evaluate clarity, completeness, organization, and usability.
  • Apply evaluation rubrics consistently across different outputs.
  • Write specific feedback that explains both the problem and how it could be improved.
  • Distinguish meaningful errors from personal stylistic preferences.

Evaluation Process

How I approach an AI output.

The exact criteria change depending on the task, but the basic evaluation process stays pretty consistent. Click each step to see what I'm looking for.

Before evaluating the response, I establish what successful completion actually requires.

  • What did the user actually ask for?
  • Who is the intended audience?
  • What requirements or constraints apply?
  • What would a strong final output need to accomplish?

I review the response against the task and evaluation criteria instead of judging it by how polished or confident it sounds.

  • Did it follow the instructions?
  • Does the structure make sense?
  • Are important requirements missing?
  • Would the intended user actually find it useful?

Then I look for defects that actually affect the quality of the output.

  • Unsupported or inaccurate claims
  • Missing information
  • Contradictions
  • Weak reasoning
  • Ambiguous instructions
  • Structural or usability problems

When a project uses a rubric, I evaluate each dimension against defined criteria and evidence.

The important part is separating a real quality problem from something I would personally write or design differently.

Useful feedback should explain more than whether something is right or wrong.

I try to identify:

  • What the problem is
  • Why it matters
  • What requirement or criterion it affects
  • What change would resolve it

Instructional Design Connection

Why L&D is useful in AI evaluation.

AI evaluation and instructional design actually use a lot of the same judgment.

In instructional design, I constantly ask whether content is accurate, appropriate for the audience, aligned to the objective, structured clearly, measurable, and usable in the environment where someone will encounter it.

Those same questions are useful when evaluating AI-generated work.

It also means I'm comfortable reviewing something that looks finished and still asking whether the underlying logic actually works.

Results

What came out of the work?

Through AI training and evaluation projects, I've contributed human judgment, annotations, content refinement, and structured feedback used to improve model responses and behavior.

The work has also given me a stronger framework for evaluating AI-generated content inside instructional design workflows instead of automatically accepting the first generated result.

Because project-level training data and performance metrics are confidential, I don't publish client or model-specific results here.

Reflection

What I've learned.

The most interesting part of AI evaluation for me is how much it depends on judgment rather than simple error checking.

Some outputs are obviously wrong. The harder cases are mostly correct.

That's where you have to distinguish between something that could be improved because of personal preference and something that has a meaningful problem with accuracy, reasoning, alignment, usability, or completeness.

That distinction has made me more thoughtful about how I use AI in my own instructional design work too. I use it as a development tool, but I don't treat generated output as finished work without review.
