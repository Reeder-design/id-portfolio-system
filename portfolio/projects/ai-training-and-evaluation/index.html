AI Training and Evaluation

My AI work focuses on evaluating whether generated content actually satisfies the task it was created to perform. That means looking beyond fluency and surface polish to examine accuracy, grounding, instruction adherence, completeness, consistency, usability, and overall task success.

I use structured criteria, analytical review, evidence-based issue detection, actionable feedback, and quality assurance to help improve model outputs and the data used to train and evaluate AI systems.

How I Approach Evaluation

Good AI evaluation starts with the task.

A response can be clear, professional, and convincing while still being wrong, incomplete, unsupported, or misaligned with what the user actually requested.

I start by understanding the task, the intended audience, the available source material or ground truth, and the criteria that define successful performance. Only then do I evaluate the output.

I also try to separate material quality issues from stylistic preference. The question isn't whether I personally would have written something differently. The question is whether the output satisfies the requirements and whether any problems meaningfully reduce its accuracy, usefulness, or reliability.

Core Evaluation Skills

Evidence before impression.

  • Interpret task instructions and constraints
  • Compare outputs against source material or reference information
  • Detect factual, logical, structural, and usability issues
  • Distinguish minor issues from material failures
  • Apply task-specific evaluation criteria consistently
  • Write feedback that explains what needs to improve and why
  • Perform final QA on the evaluation itself

Evaluation Mindset

The score is the end of the reasoning process, not the beginning.

Strong evaluation requires understanding why an output succeeds or fails. A rating should be supported by observable evidence and applied consistently against the criteria for that task.

1

Understand the Task

Identify the requested output, intended audience, explicit instructions, constraints, required format, and the purpose the output is supposed to serve.

2

Establish the Evaluation Standard

Determine what evidence is available and what quality means for this particular task. Depending on the assignment, that may include a source document, reference answer, rubric, factual ground truth, or explicit task requirements.

3

Review the Output Independently

Examine what the model actually produced without filling in missing information, overlooking unsupported claims, or giving credit for content that is only implied.

4

Identify + Classify Issues

Determine what is wrong, where the issue occurs, what evaluation criterion it affects, and whether the problem is minor, major, or severe enough to undermine task success.

5

Score Against the Criteria

Translate the evidence into ratings using defined standards rather than an overall impression of whether the response simply "looks good."

6

Write Diagnostic Feedback

Explain what the problem is, why it matters, and what a stronger output should do differently. Useful feedback supports improvement rather than merely assigning judgment.

7

QA the Evaluation

Recheck the ratings, comments, labels, and supporting evidence for internal consistency. In larger evaluation programs, calibration and reviewer comparison can also help keep standards consistent across evaluators.

Quality + Data Integrity

Evaluation should be reproducible.

If two evaluators are working from the same task, evidence, and standards, their conclusions should be reasonably consistent. That requires clear criteria, documented reasoning, disciplined review, and attention to data quality.

I think of QA as part of the evaluation itself: verifying that the issue identified is actually supported, that the severity matches its impact, that the score matches the written feedback, and that the final evaluation follows the requested structure.

AI + Instructional Design

The disciplines overlap more than they first appear.

Instructional design and AI evaluation both require translating a goal into observable standards for quality.

In learning design, I ask what someone should know or be able to do and how I can determine whether the learning experience supports that outcome.

In AI evaluation, I ask what the model was supposed to produce, what evidence demonstrates success, where the output falls short, and what feedback would improve the next result.

Shared Skills

Judgment supported by structure.

  • Define expected performance
  • Build useful evaluation criteria
  • Analyze complex information
  • Detect gaps and inconsistencies
  • Give specific feedback
  • Use iteration to improve quality
