The Workshop

I facilitated a live MEDDPICC sales workshop for real seller and partner audiences.

The workshop was part of a broader sales-enablement effort. I facilitated assigned modules for internal sellers and external partners in both U.S. and international sessions, using the prepared materials as the backbone while making the live experience responsive to the people in the room.

Experienced sellers

Many learners already had deep sales experience, so I made room for useful examples and discussion instead of over-explaining basic concepts.

Mixed audiences

Internal teams and external partners brought different levels of context, so I had to explain the framework clearly without assuming the same background knowledge.

Global delivery

U.S. and international audiences meant I had to use clear language, avoid overly local examples, and check that the discussion still landed across different markets and experience levels.

How I Facilitated

I used participation to find out what the audience actually understood.

These were not hypothetical facilitation techniques. They were the moves I used during the workshop to keep the sessions active and useful.

Diagnose

Ask before telling.

I used questions to hear the group’s current interpretation before adding more explanation.

What I did

I used targeted prompts before giving the answer or definition.

Why it mattered

Signal I watched

How I Adapted

I changed the delivery when the audience told me the first version was not enough.

What I read from the room

The concept or distinction was not stable yet.

What I did next

I slowed down, reframed the concept, and tested it again with a concrete seller example.

What I Brought Back

The live sessions gave me direct evidence for future learning design.

Because I was hearing learner questions firsthand, I could see where future self-paced content, scenarios, checks, or facilitator guidance needed more support.

Recurring questions

I used repeated questions as a signal that future content needed a clearer explanation or reference example.

Common misconceptions

I treated recurring misunderstandings as candidates for better scenario distractors, feedback, and formative checks.

Strong learner examples

I paid attention to the seller examples that made the framework feel credible and transferable across audiences.

Timing friction

I noted where explanations or activities took too long so future deliveries could protect more time for application and debrief.
